# GameSpot Review: Motor City Online

**Source:** GameSpot  
**Date:** November 21, 2001  
**Reviewer:** Stephen Poole  
**Score:** 7.6/10  
**Archived:** <https://www.gamespot.com/reviews/motor-city-online-review/1900-2826690/>

---

## Overview

GameSpot's Stephen Poole reviewed Motor City Online one month after its October 2001 launch, awarding it a 7.6/10 — a "good" rating that acknowledged both the game's ambitious vision and its significant flaws.

## Key Excerpts

### The Vision
> *"When Electronic Arts first announced plans for Need for Speed: Motor City two years ago, it sounded like a racing buff's dream--and for good reason. Imagine a persistent-world gameworld such as Ultima Online or EverQuest, blended with the high-powered action of the Need for Speed series and soaked in the atmosphere invoked by movies like American Graffiti and Two Lane Blacktop..."*

### Documentation Issues
> *"The game carries a $40 sticker price, and for your money you get a flashy box, a jewel case, a CD, and a month's free play--but no manual. All the documentation is accessed online either from the MCO software or the MCO Web site, and the process of getting answers to specific questions can be tedious even for those with broadband access."*

> *"Also, some info from the MCO 'knowledge base' is already outdated. For instance, it says you can have up to three personas per server, when you actually can have only one."*

### Starter Cars
> *"Why are you given only three pathetic choices--a Bel-Air, a Fairlane, and a Ranchero (all '57s)--for your first vehicle? Because you can actually race and earn money without owning a car (more on that later), the wise person might try to save the $8,000-plus these death traps cost and sock it away toward a real car, but no dice: You must buy one."*

### The Sponsored Racing Problem
> *"Theoretically, the fastest way to build a bankroll is to run open races because the purses can be larger, but hardly anyone is willing to do it."*

> *"Time after time we tried to start an open street race, but no one would join and no one would create one."*

> *"So what happens is that many players tend to run sponsored circuit races... or, even better, sponsored time trials. These are an easy way to rack up cash quickly--no waiting on humans to join and select cars, and you can easily pick up between $150 and $200 every two or three minutes with a decent showing."*

> *"Instead, to gain cash more quickly, the vast majority of beginning players will run sponsored time trials--again, in common cars with more forgiving physics, which makes it even harder to get acclimated to the more realistic handling of personal cars when you finally make enough money to get a better car you feel comfortable racing."*

### Latency Issues
> *"When a broadband user squares off against opponents with dial-up connections (or, even worse, an AOL dial-up connection), there's no telling what sort of magic will take place--cars disappearing and reappearing, bouncing up and down on the track as though it were a trampoline, and sometimes shooting from behind to win a race when you thought you'd left them in the dust long before."*

> *"There are currently four MCO servers where you can have a persona, but when you log on you aren't shown what your latency is to each one."*

### Auction System
> *"The auction interface is rather clunky: There are six auctions run by the service and one user-based auction, and after checking out one you're returned to the main menu and must start all over. Another problem with the auction interface is its small screens--to view all 197 cars being auctioned by users on one server, we had to scroll through nine very laggy screens."*

> *"The lone Mustang we saw being auctioned was running upward of $267,000, and while we saw users online with $2 million or so in the bank, it's hard to imagine an average player coming up with this kind of money."*

### The Addiction
> *"Despite all these issues, a funny thing happened on the way to the finish line. Despite a nonracing interface that's unwieldy and cluttered... there are times when MCO just sucks you in. It's especially tempting on the weekends, when the servers are heavily populated and there's always somebody ready to run... You can spend hours trying to scrape up enough cash to juice up your wretched Ranchero."*

### Final Assessment
> *"MCO probably won't satisfy the Grand Prix Legends or NASCAR 4 crowd accustomed to near-perfect authenticity in their racing games, because EA took some creative license in determining car weights in an attempt to balance gameplay. But there's no denying that MCO theoretically lets you build a screamer that's pure fun to drive--just don't plan to earn one in a sitting or two. If you're into racing and, perhaps more importantly, into trying to find bargains to squeeze more horsepower or handling out of a custom-built ride, you'll probably find it easy to look past MCO's shortcomings and immerse yourself in its wild, weird world."*

## Preservation Context

Stephen Poole's review is one of the most detailed contemporary assessments of MCO's launch state. Several observations are particularly notable for the historical record:

- **No manual in the box:** Documentation was entirely online, and already outdated at launch (claiming 3 personas per server when only 1 was allowed)
- **The sponsored racing economy:** Poole identified the core gameplay loop problem — players gravitated to sponsored races for safe income, undermining the "own your car" fantasy
- **Server count:** Four MCO servers at launch (this would later expand)
- **Auction prices:** $267,000 for a Mustang, while top players had $2 million banked — revealing the extreme economic stratification
- **The weekend effect:** Servers were "heavily populated" on weekends, suggesting a dedicated but small core community

The review's closing line — "immerse yourself in its wild, weird world" — captures the strange charm that would keep a small but passionate player base engaged until the August 2003 shutdown.
