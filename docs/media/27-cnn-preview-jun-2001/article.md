## Overview

Six months before Motor City Online's eventual October 2001 release, GamePro.com (syndicated through CNN) published a detailed preview of the game, capturing it at a pivotal moment in development when beta testing was about to begin.

## Key Details

### Development History
The article notes the game had undergone "name changes and concept re-inventions, engine overhauls and near-death experiences" during its long development cycle.

### Car Roster
- Nearly **60 base model cars** at launch (from the 1930s through the 1970s)
- Licensed from **Ford, Chrysler, and Chevrolet**
- Over **1,000 customizable parts**
- More cars planned for post-launch release

### Online Structure
- **Shard system** similar to Ultima Online
- Up to **five personas per shard**
- Each persona has their own bankroll, garage, and community loyalties

### Game Modes
- **Sim World:** The persistent online universe with car trading, betting, salaries, and racing
- **Arcade Mode:** Casual racing with no stakes
- **Auction system:** eBay-like player auctions for cars and parts
- **Club system:** With turf wars, rankings, and leaderboards

### Graphics and Physics
- Enhanced version of the Need for Speed engine
- All-new **4-point physics engine** developed specifically for MCO
- Dynamic weather effects including rain and headlights
- Different cars feel distinct (weight, handling)

### Development Timeline
- **Beta start:** June 11, 2001 (as of this article)
- **Expected release:** "Later this summer" (2001)
- *Note: The game ultimately shipped in October 2001, roughly four months later than the summer target mentioned here.*

## Preservation Context

This preview captures the game during its final development push, just before the open beta period that would last through summer and early fall 2001. The enthusiasm in the article reflects the anticipation surrounding what was then positioned as "the first online racing universe."

The preview's emphasis on community features, persistent economics, and social gameplay would prove prescient — these were indeed the defining characteristics of the final product, even if technical issues at launch would later draw criticism from reviewers.
